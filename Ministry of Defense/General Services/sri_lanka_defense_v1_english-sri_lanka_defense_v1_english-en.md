**Sri Lanka Defence: A General Overview**

Prepared October 2026  |  General information summary

## **1. Introduction**

Sri Lanka's defence system exists to protect the sovereignty, territorial integrity and national security of the island. Since independence in 1948, the armed forces have grown from small colonial-era units into a professional three-service force. Defence policy is shaped by the island's strategic position in the Indian Ocean, close to some of the world's busiest shipping lanes, and by the experience of an internal conflict of nearly three decades that ended in May 2009.

## **2. Political and Command Structure**

The President is Head of State and Commander-in-Chief of the Armed Forces. President Anura Kumara Dissanayake also holds the Defence portfolio.

Deputy Minister of Defence: Major General Aruna Jayasekara (Retd). Secretary to the Ministry: Air Vice Marshal Sampath Thuyacontha (Retd).

The Ministry of Defence formulates and coordinates defence policy and oversees the day-to-day running of the armed forces, contingency planning and defence procurement.

The Minister of Defence and the Defence Secretary are permanent members of the National Security Council.

## **3. The Armed Forces**

The Army is currently led by Lieutenant General Nilantha Premaratne, who became its 26th Commander on 1 August 2026.

## **4. Other Defence Institutions**

The Civil Security Department, the Sri Lanka Coast Guard, the National Cadet Corps, General Sir John Kotelawala Defence University, the Defence Services Command and Staff College, the Department of Meteorology and the Disaster Management Division operate under the Ministry of Defence. Law enforcement is handled separately by the Sri Lanka Police, under the Ministry of Public Security.

## **5. Defence Spending**

Defence is one of the largest items in the national budget. The allocation was about Rs. 440 billion in 2025, and the Ministry of Defence stated in August 2026 that Rs. 530 billion has been allocated for 2026. Most spending is recurrent (salaries, operations and maintenance), with a smaller share for procurement. Troop numbers have been reduced significantly since 2009, while budget allocations have risen.

## **6. Current Priorities**

Professionalism and training: the President has called for forces equipped with professional and scientific expertise, with special attention to overseas training.

Engineering and development services: using the forces' engineering corps to reduce delays and excess costs in government construction projects.

Maritime security: sea surveillance, coastal protection, curbing smuggling and illegal fishing, and search and rescue.

Disaster response: humanitarian relief and rescue during natural disasters.

International engagement: UN peacekeeping, training exchanges and bilateral defence cooperation, under a neutral, non-aligned foreign policy.

Modernisation: renewing ageing equipment within fiscal limits.

## **7. Key Challenges**

Fiscal constraints during post-crisis economic recovery; ageing equipment; maritime and transnational crime; cyber and emerging threats; and climate-related disasters.

*This is a general summary based on public sources (Ministry of Defence, Sri Lanka; President's Office; budget documents) as of October 2026. Figures and appointments may change.*
